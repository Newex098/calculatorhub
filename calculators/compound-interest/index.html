What is Compound Interest?

Compound interest is the interest earned on both your initial principal investment and the accumulated interest from prior periods. Often referred to by financial educators as "interest on interest," compounding allows your savings and investments to grow at an accelerating, exponential rate over time rather than a flat linear progression.

When you deposit money into an interest-bearing account—such as a fixed deposit, bonds, or dividend-reinvesting mutual funds—your earned interest is added back to your balance. In subsequent periods, the next round of interest is computed on that higher total balance, compounding your earnings cycle after cycle.

How Compound Interest Works

The compounding mechanism operates on three critical pillars:

  • Principal Amount (P): The initial capital you start with. Larger principal amounts generate higher initial interest payouts.
  • Interest Rate (r): The annual percentage yield paid on your funds. Even fractional increases in interest rates compound into substantial sums over long horizons.
  • Compounding Frequency (n): How often earned interest is credited to the principal (annually, half-yearly, quarterly, monthly, or daily). More frequent compounding boosts final yields.
  • Time Horizon (t): The total duration your capital remains invested. Time is the most powerful variable in compound interest because exponential curves steepen significantly over decades.

The Compound Interest Formula

The standard mathematical equation used across global financial systems to calculate compound interest is:

A = P × (1 + r / n)^(n × t)

Total Compound Interest: CI = A − P

Where each variable represents:

  • A: Final maturity amount (Future Value)
  • P: Initial principal investment
  • r: Annual nominal interest rate expressed as a decimal (e.g. 8% = 0.08)
  • n: Number of compounding periods per year (1 for Annual, 2 for Half-Yearly, 4 for Quarterly, 12 for Monthly, 365 for Daily)
  • t: Total investment horizon in years (decimal values like 2.5 are fully supported)

Handling Fractional-Year Periods

For fractional-year periods (such as 2.5 years or 18 months), CalculatorHub uses the standard exponential compounding convention A = P(1 + r/n)^(n × t) across the exact decimal duration. In the yearly growth table above, full consecutive years are displayed first, followed by a designated final fractional row detailing the exact partial period.

Difference Between Simple and Compound Interest

While both formulas compute interest on money, their fundamental mechanics differ substantially:

Feature Simple Interest (SI) Compound Interest (CI)
Calculation Base Original principal balance only Principal + all accumulated prior interest
Formula SI = P × r × t A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)
Growth Curve Linear (constant annual gain) Exponential (accelerating annual gain)
Annual Interest Earned Identical every single year Increases every compounding period
Best Suited For Short-term personal loans, auto loans Long-term investments, FDs, mutual funds

Step-by-Step Worked Example

Example: ₹10,000 invested at 8% annual interest for 5 years compounded annually
  • 1
    Identify Inputs: Principal P = ₹10,000, Rate r = 0.08, Frequency n = 1, Time t = 5.
  • 2
    Substitute in Formula: A = 10,000 × (1 + 0.08 / 1)^(1 × 5) = 10,000 × (1.08)^5.
  • 3
    Compute Exponential Multiplier: (1.08)^5 ≈ 1.469328.
  • 4
    Calculate Final Maturity Value: A = 10,000 × 1.469328 = ₹14,693.28.
  • 5
    Determine Total Interest Earned: CI = ₹14,693.28 − ₹10,000 = ₹4,693.28 (a 46.93% net gain).

Compound interest is the interest calculated on both the initial principal deposit and all accumulated interest from prior compounding periods. This "interest on interest" allows investments to grow exponentially over time rather than in flat linear steps.
Compound interest is calculated using the formula A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt), where A is the total maturity value, P is the initial principal, r is the annual interest rate as a decimal, n is compounding frequency per year, and t is the duration in years. Total interest earned equals A − P.
The global formula is A = P × (1 + r/n)^(nt). For annual compounding where n = 1, this simplifies to A = P(1 + r)^t.
For investors and savers, monthly compounding is better because interest is credited and begins generating its own return twelve times per year instead of once, resulting in higher final maturity values.
When the interest rate is 0%, no interest accumulates. The future maturity value equals the original principal amount (A = P), and total interest earned is ₹0.
Simple interest is paid only on the initial principal (SI = P × r × t) and yields the exact same rupee amount every year. Compound interest earns returns on both principal and past interest, creating compounding growth that accelerates over time.
